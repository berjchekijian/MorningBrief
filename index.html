Not the Pollution Exclusion: How Occupational Disease Language Is Quietly Draining PFAS Coverage

Client alertWebsite article1,200–1,500 wordsManufacturers with PFAS exposure, risk managers, coverage counselEvergreen with a fresh peg
The pegFire-Dex LLC v. Admiral Insurance Co., No. 1:23-cv-01612 (N.D. Ohio), decided September 4 — Judge Brennan held that an occupational-disease exclusion in two CGL policies bars any duty to defend the PFAS suits over firefighter turnout gear.
The anglePFAS defense strategy across the industry has been built on an assumption that CGL towers will fund the defense while the science is litigated. The exclusion fights everyone anticipated were pollution exclusions, argued on the familiar sudden-and-accidental and total-pollution grounds. This ruling did not turn on any of that. It turned on occupational disease — language that sweeps in any claim by a worker exposed on the job, which describes a very large share of the PFAS docket in gear, textiles, coatings and AFFF-adjacent equipment. The second-order point is the one worth publishing: a manufacturer auditing its coverage for pollution language is reading the wrong page, and the exclusion that decides the case may be one nobody flagged. Close with a specific instruction — pull the occupational-disease and bodily-injury-to-workers language from every layer of the tower, not just the pollution endorsements.
Why it works: it tells readers to go look at a specific paragraph in a document they already have, which converts to calls better than almost anything else. It also puts the firm at the coverage/toxic-tort seam rather than in the crowded PFAS-update lane.

The Second Wave Is the Payer: Medicare Secondary Payer Claims Arrive After the Global Settlement

Client alertLinkedIn800 wordsMass tort defendants, settlement counsel, in-house litigation leadershipShelf life 12+ months
The pegHumana sued Monsanto/Bayer in the District of New Jersey on September 4 to recover Medicare Advantage costs paid for members allegedly injured by Roundup, seeking double damages under the Medicare Secondary Payer Act — filed while Bayer pursues approval of a reported $7.25 billion global settlement.
The angleDefendants model mass tort exposure as the tort docket plus the settlement, and treat a global deal as the end of the number. MSP Act claims by Medicare Advantage organizations are not released by settlements with individual claimants, carry a private right of action with double damages, and tend to arrive precisely when a defendant believes it has bought peace. The insight most coverage will miss is about sequencing: the payer suit is filed because a global settlement is near, since the settlement establishes both the injury universe and the defendant's willingness to pay. Any client heading toward global resolution in talc, PFAS, hair relaxer or device litigation should be pricing an MSP tail into the deal rather than discovering it afterward.
Why it works: it reframes a familiar story for the exact audience that makes outside-counsel decisions, and the takeaway — price the MSP tail into the settlement — is concrete, contrarian, and arrives before most of the market is thinking about it.

PFOA and PFOS Are Hazardous Substances, Full Stop: What the D.C. Circuit Ruling Does to Allocation

Client alert900–1,100 wordsIndustrial property owners, PRPs, remediation counsel, PFAS supply chainShelf life 12+ months
The pegChamber of Commerce of the United States v. EPA, No. 24-1193 (D.C. Cir., decided August 18, 2026) — a unanimous panel upheld EPA's CERCLA § 102(a) designation of PFOA and PFOS against every industry challenge, including the § 102(a) interpretation, the notice-and-comment process and the cost-benefit analysis.
The angleMost write-ups will report that the designation survived. The useful piece is about what changes downstream now that it is settled rather than contingent. Two things: drinking-water MCLs move toward functioning as ARARs in remediation, which raises cleanup standards at sites that were scoped on the assumption the designation might fall; and allocation among PRPs now runs against parties who never manufactured PFAS at all — passive landowners, downstream users, publicly owned treatment works receiving industrial influent. The genuinely contrarian note is that the panel itself cautioned its relevance to the parallel drinking-water rule challenge "should not be overstated," which is a line defense counsel should be quoting rather than letting the other side treat this as having decided more than it did.
Why it works: it names a case, a court, a statutory provision and a specific practical consequence, and it hands the reader a quotable limiting line from the opinion. That combination is what makes an alert useful to another lawyer rather than merely informative.

649 Generators and One Question: Clean Air Act Aggregation at Co-Located Data Centers

Website article1,000–1,200 wordsData center developers, hyperscaler counsel, utilities, siting and permitting teamsShelf life 18 months
The pegNorth Carolina DEQ approved air permits on September 1 for 592 emergency generators serving an Amazon data center and 57 temporary Duke Energy units on the same Richmond County site, reviewed as separate projects with non-combined emissions modeling. The Southern Environmental Law Center's July 30 comment letter argues the co-located generators are a single Clean Air Act stationary source.
The angleSingle-source aggregation determines whether a project is a minor source or triggers PSD and Title V — and therefore the entire permitting posture, the schedule, and whether there is a hook for later citizen-suit and nuisance litigation. It is being decided right now, permit by permit, in state agencies with no national rule and little consistency, and the pattern being set will govern every hyperscaler-plus-utility parcel for years. The second-order point: aggregation is not merely a permitting problem. A finding that the generators are one source creates an emissions record that plaintiffs will later use in diesel particulate exposure claims against the operator. Permitting posture and toxic tort posture turn out to be the same decision, made years apart.
Why it works: the firm is building a data center practice, and this is the sophisticated question rather than the moratorium-tracker content everyone else is producing. It pairs naturally with the September 7 edition's emergency-ordinance piece — procedure and permitting as the two fast attacks — and demonstrates the environmental-plus-toxic-tort combination that is the firm's actual differentiator.
